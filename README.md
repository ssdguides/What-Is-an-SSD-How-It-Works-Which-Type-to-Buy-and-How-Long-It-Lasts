Every computer needs permanent storage, because memory (RAM) forgets everything when the power goes off. A solid-state drive (SSD) does that job with flash memory chips and no moving parts, while a hard disk drive (HDD) uses spinning platters and a moving head. That one difference is why an SSD upgrade is often the single most noticeable improvement you can make to an older PC.

How an SSD works

Data is stored as electrical charge in NAND flash cells. A controller chip manages everything: it talks to your computer, decides where data lands, runs error correction, and keeps a translation map between the addresses your PC uses and the physical spots on the flash. Flash can’t overwrite data in place, so a changed file goes to fresh pages and the old ones are cleaned up later. Behind the scenes, wear leveling spreads writes evenly, and TRIM lets the operating system tell the drive which data was deleted.

The types, and a common mix-up

Three things get confused: the form factor (shape), the interface (connector) and the protocol (language).

SATA SSD (2.5-inch): works in almost any PC, topping out near 600 MB/s.
M.2 SATA: a small card that’s no faster than a 2.5-inch SATA drive.
M.2 NVMe: runs over PCIe and is much faster. PCIe Gen 3 x4 tops out near 3.9 GB/s, Gen 4 near 7.9 GB/s and Gen 5 near 15.8 GB/s.
External SSD: USB or Thunderbolt. Speed depends on the drive, the cable and your port.

M.2 is only the shape. Check your laptop or motherboard manual for supported protocol, length and PCIe generation before buying.

Details that explain performance

NAND type: SLC, MLC, TLC and QLC store 1, 2, 3 or 4 bits per cell. More bits means lower cost but lower endurance and slower sustained writes. TLC is the mainstream balance.
DRAM cache: helps heavy random workloads, but a well-designed DRAM-less drive can feel perfectly fast for everyday use.
Controller: the drive’s brain. Two drives with similar NAND can behave very differently.

Lifespan

NAND wears with writes, but normal users rarely reach the limit. TBW (terabytes written) and DWPD (drive writes per day) are manufacturer ratings. Warranty, typically 3–5 years, is a guarantee, not a prediction. Keep free space, leave TRIM on, and don’t defragment.

Checking your drive in Windows

Open Task Manager, go to Performance, then Disk, and the drive type is listed at the top right. For health, tools like CrystalDiskInfo or your manufacturer’s utility show SMART data. SMART is a useful early warning, but a “Good” status doesn’t replace backups.

The verdict

If your computer still boots from a hard drive, upgrading to any reputable SSD is the best performance change you can make. Confirm your slot and protocol first, pick enough capacity, and keep a backup.
